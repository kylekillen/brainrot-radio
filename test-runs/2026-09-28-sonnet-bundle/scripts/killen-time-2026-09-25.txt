[BASIL] AI and Technology. The Specter of Neuralese, on Astral Codex Ten. Background: earlier this month TechCrunch reported that OpenAI's new reasoning technique was alarming AI safety experts. The technique is recurrence, and the fight is over whether it is the thing the AI 2027 scenario warned about.

[BROOKE] Alexander starts with how a transformer works. A modern frontier model has something like a hundred layers. A word goes in at the bottom, gets processed through the layers, and a predicted next word comes out the top. If a problem needs more than a hundred layers of thinking, then from 2017 to 2024, in his words, the answer was "you're screwed." From 2024 on, the answer was chain of thought: the model writes its intermediate result as text on a scratchpad, then runs the transformer again on that text, and repeats.

[BASIL] He points out this was purely a capabilities play, but it happened to be great for safety, because the scratchpad is in English. You can read what the AI is thinking. His example is the model thinking "Better hack some websites, then kill all humans," and you shut it down. He adds a caveat: if thousands of AIs are writing millions of pages of scratchpad, you can't read all of it in real time, so you delegate to fallible AI monitors. But in theory it works.

[BROOKE] Now the bottleneck. Language, he writes, is slower and shallower than thought. The model sends delicate ideas from layer to layer at the speed of light for a hundred steps, and then it has to encode them into, quote, "twenty-six glyphs invented by Phoenician turquoise miners in 1800 BC," and then decode them again. So the idea: finish the hundred layers, then send the resulting thought straight back to the first layer, without writing it down. That's neuralese recurrence. Recurrence because it loops, neuralese because the thing looping is the thought itself, in the model's native language, a vector thousands of numbers long. We don't know how to read those, and Alexander notes that reading them belongs to interpretability, which he says is still in its infancy.

[BASIL] So did OpenAI do it? Their chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, says "not really." The claim, as Alexander quotes it, is that the depth of the computation graph is within a factor of two of GPT-4. Alexander's first job is to explain why that could sound reassuring. If you loop layers three and two of a four-layer model a few times, the data gets eight layers of processing. That's no worse, from an alignment view, than just building an eight-layer transformer, which was always allowed. He gives rough numbers with a big "let's say": GPT-2 had 48 layers, GPT-3 had 96, GPT-4 maybe 120, and a looped model might be doing the equivalent of 180.

[BROOKE] Then he asks the obvious follow-up. If looping is so free, why not loop a million times? His answer has two parts. First, looped layers are a hack. A real new layer gives the model more room to store knowledge and thought styles; a looped layer just reuses the same knowledge more times. He asked Claude Fable to estimate the capability gain in "generations," GPT-4 to GPT-5 sized steps, and got zero for chain of thought as a baseline, plus 0.03 for doubling simulated layers by looping, and plus 0.25 for doubling real layers. He's upfront that an AI researcher who proofread the post called those estimates "bad," but says nobody has better ones.

[BASIL] Second part, and I think the more important one: there's a budget. More compute per chain-of-thought token means fewer tokens. Alexander infers OpenAI decided the balance lay at looping something like two to four times, not a thousand. And true unbounded looping isn't something anyone can currently train. There's no internet text of humans reasoning entirely in vectors, and training a vector-only thought process for thousands of steps without ever surfacing into chain-of-thought is, quote, "prohibitively costly." That, he says, is what Pachocki was denying: "don't worry, we still don't know how to do that."

[BROOKE] But he says that isn't entirely reassuring, and he lays out three levels. One: a limited number of layers, which can't plot without writing the plot on the scratchpad. That's every previous transformer. Two: a looped transformer, which can simulate a lot of layers and make a plot within a single forward pass between scratchpad steps. He says OpenAI's Astra model is getting part of the way to that. Three: true neuralese, no chain of thought at all, where the model can plot at leisure and we'd never know. His line on level three: the dangerous AIs in AI 2027 are that, "but so far nobody has invented this in real life."

[BASIL] So the practical question becomes a policy one, and here he leans on a post by Linchuan Zhang, titled Categorical Taboos Are Much Better Than Threshold Taboos. The analogy is alcohol. [BROOKE] And he says we don't currently agree on what the taboo even is.
